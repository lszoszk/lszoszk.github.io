teaching

Workshops on AI and data for human rights professionals, university courses at Adam Mickiewicz University, and interactive materials for class.

I teach lawyers and human rights professionals to work with AI and data by building small tools on the documents they already use, such as UN reports, General Comments and court judgments. No coding experience is needed, and participants build their first tool during the workshop itself.

What I learned teaching vibe-coding to 16 human rights professionals (Venice, 2025)
  1. The exposure gap is large. Only one of 16 participants had used a paid language model; most judged what AI can do by the free versions.
  2. Purpose before process. The most frequent complaint was “I didn't understand why we were building this”. A workshop should therefore start with the problem (why this is better than Ctrl+F).
  3. Seeing it work is the turning point. For participants without a technical background, a working demo has to come before the explanation.
  4. The outcomes are bimodal. About 38% got it at once and 25% stayed lost, with little in between. AI tools do not flatten the learning curve; they change its shape.
  5. Too many applications confuse more than the concepts. Participants were not lost in programming but in switching between Cursor, Colab and the terminal, so fewer tools work better.
  6. Natural language is not natural to everyone. Prompting still requires structured thinking, and some participants struggle with it in Python and in prompts alike.
